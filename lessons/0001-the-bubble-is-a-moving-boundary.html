The bubble is a moving boundary

A bubble’s radius can change only by moving the liquid around it. That liquid supplies the inertia in the simplest cavitation model.

Picture one nearly spherical bubble in liquid. The gas or vapor inside has pressure pB; liquid far away has pressure p∞. We describe the boundary by its radius R(t). When R grows, liquid must flow outward; when R shrinks, liquid flows inward. The bubble is therefore a moving interface, not a small solid bead with a fixed mass.

One balance worth keeping

For a stationary, clean, spherical bubble, surface tension holds the interface curved. The inside pressure must be higher than the outside pressure by

pB − p∞ = 2σ / R.

Here σ is surface tension. A pressure difference by itself therefore does not prove the bubble is growing. With water’s approximate room-temperature surface tension, σ ≈ 0.072 N/m, a 5 μm bubble can sit at rest with an inside-over-outside pressure difference of about 29 kPa.

The dynamic idea

If the outward pressure drive exceeds what surface tension and other resistance can balance, the liquid accelerates outward. When the drive later weakens or the outside pressure rises, the moving liquid does not stop instantly: its inertia can carry the bubble farther before collapse begins. The same liquid then accelerates inward. Gas compression, vapor condensation, and dissipation influence the final collapse and possible rebound.

The static relation above is a starting point. A moving bubble also needs liquid inertia and viscous stress in its equation of motion. A real nanodroplet can add a shell, phase change, and nearby boundaries; we will add those only when the simpler picture is secure.
